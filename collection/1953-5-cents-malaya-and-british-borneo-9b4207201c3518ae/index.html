Historical background

01A dollar shared across the sea

In Singapore and the Malay States the idea of sharing money had a history older than this coin. The Straits Settlements dollar was also accepted in the Malay States and parts of Borneo. A board formed in 1938 brought the Malayan dollar into use in 1941. Japan’s invasion interrupted its rollout, and the occupying authorities issued military notes, often called banana notes. The Malayan dollar returned after the war.

The later board widened the arrangement. The Monetary Authority of Singapore lists Singapore, the Federation of Malaya, Brunei, Sabah and Sarawak as participants. Museums Victoria records a London-struck example dated 1953. Singapore’s central bank places the joint issue in 1954. The two dates describe different things, the year on the coin and the year used in the account of the shared currency’s introduction.

[1][3]

02A crowned portrait and a large 5

Elizabeth II takes up most of the front, even with her name spelled out around her. Thomas’s portrait makes the crown part of her outline. The five cents on the other side is just as assertive. Its lines run inside a numeral large enough to recognize at a glance, leaving the beaded ring to separate it from the name of the issuing area.

The two faces tell different parts of the story. The portrait identifies the sovereign, while the names on the other side identify the shared monetary area. The board’s full name is much longer than the coin’s legend. A person buying something for five cents needed the value most of all.

[1][2]

03When the shared dollar gave way

The shared currency remained in use as political arrangements in the region changed. Singapore, Malaysia and Brunei each introduced their own money in 1967. This small piece comes from an earlier phase, when different administrations could share a circulating dollar. The place names around its rim are as useful for understanding it as the date.

[3]

Identification notes

The unlettered 1953 issue

The crowned portrait, round outline, beaded numeral and date match NU 27173. In this series, H and KN appear below CENTS on certain later issues. The 1953 listings are unlettered, and the exact-date working strike and proof at Museums Victoria are both attributed to London. The small marks beside the date are separate from that mintmark position. Finish remains open. The dimensions above describe the type and the museum’s specimen.

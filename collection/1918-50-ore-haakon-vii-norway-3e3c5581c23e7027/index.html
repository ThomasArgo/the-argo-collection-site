50 Ore - Haakon VII

1918 · Kingdom of Norway · Haakon VII

Haakon VII’s name runs above his portrait, and his promise runs below it. This silver half-krone was struck thirteen years after Norway left its union with Sweden, in the last year of a war that reached Norwegian sailors even though their country stayed neutral.

1918 Kongsberg issue, Brekke 49. Overdate unconfirmed.

Historical background

01How Carl became Haakon

On 7 June 1905 the Norwegian parliament declared the union with Sweden dissolved. Once the separation had been negotiated, Norway still had to decide how it wanted to be governed. The choice of a king fell on Prince Carl of Denmark, a naval officer married to Princess Maud, the daughter of Britain’s Edward VII. Carl asked to see a popular vote before accepting.

The vote backed the offer of the throne and parliament elected Carl in November. He took the name Haakon and his son Alexander became Olav, names with a long history in Norway. The coin’s wording fits that decision. It gives him a Norwegian royal name, names the country he serves, and puts his motto within sight of anyone making change.

[2]

02Neutral on land, exposed at sea

Norway remained neutral during the First World War, but its merchant ships still carried cargo through waters where submarines and mines could sink them. The Norwegian royal household’s account of the armistice centenary remembers the merchant sailors who died. That history gives the date on this coin a wider setting than the reign alone.

The coin itself is a regular half-krone. There is no battle, ship, or armistice date on either face. A shopkeeper could count two of these to make one krone in 1918, just as with earlier dates of the design. The contrast between what people lived through and what stayed familiar in their pockets is part of its appeal.

[3][5]

03The lion and the axe

The axe is easy to miss on a worn piece. It belongs to the lion in Norway’s arms and recalls Saint Olav, the medieval king associated with the Christianization of Norway. The heraldic image was already centuries old when Haakon became king. Putting it behind a new monarch’s portrait gave this little coin two different time scales.

The arms stand upright and fill most of the center. The smaller knots make a frame, leaving the denomination easy to find underneath. I can identify their threefold shape from the comparison, but I have left their meaning open.

[1][4]

Identification notes

What the date can tell us

The portrait, motto, shield and value match the silver Kongsberg type. The date reads 1918. The catalog records an 18/12 overdate, which would require checking for remnants of the earlier digit under magnification. I’ve left the die variety open. The weight and diameter here are published type figures.

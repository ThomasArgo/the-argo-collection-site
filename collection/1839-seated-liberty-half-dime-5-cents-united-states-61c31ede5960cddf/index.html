Historical background

01Five cents before the nickel

In the 1837 coinage law the half dime sat at the small end of a family of silver coins. A dollar divided into two half dollars, four quarters, ten dimes or twenty half dimes. The half dime took its name from that arithmetic. Its legal weight, 20⅝ grains, was one-twentieth of the silver dollar's 412½ grains, and both coins had the same silver fineness. A set of twenty half dimes therefore represented the same nominal amount of standard silver as one dollar, before allowances for the small variations of manufacture.

That proportional relationship explains the size of the coin better than its face value alone. A small silver denomination had to fit its place in the weight system. The wreath fills the available field neatly, with HALF on one line and DIME on the next. Today five cents brings to mind the larger nickel. Reading the words on this reverse makes the older system easier to picture.

[1][3]

02Liberty takes a seat

The seated figure was Christian Gobrecht’s design for a generation of United States silver coins. She leans against a shield and holds a liberty pole topped by a cap. The pose lets a tiny coin carry both the seated figure and two distinct symbols. The outline of the cap and the shield still do much of that work even where the finer lines of the garment have disappeared.

The first Philadelphia half dimes of this design, struck in 1837, had open space around Liberty. The mint added thirteen stars to the design in 1838. On this 1839 coin the stars bring the portrait into line with other Seated Liberty issues, while the reverse continues to name the country and the value around a wreath. The early portrait also predates the extra fold of cloth added under Liberty’s arm in 1840. That little fold matters to collectors because it marks a change in the design, even though it made no difference to what the coin could buy.

[1][2]

03Hard money and paper promises

This date falls in the unsettled years after the Panic of 1837. Banks had suspended payment in specie, the contemporary term for gold or silver coin. A banknote was a claim on its issuer, and when banks stopped exchanging their notes for coin on demand, the difference between paper and hard money became immediate. Andrew Jackson’s August 1837 letter to Martin Van Buren complains about the suspension and the low-value paper substitutes people were being asked to use.

The Treasury instructed its officers to stop placing public money with deposit banks once those banks suspended specie payments. The distinction between a bank’s promise and a silver coin mattered even to the government. This half dime belonged to the federal silver standard. Its wear gives no clue where it changed hands.

[3][4][5]

Identification notes

Philadelphia and New Orleans

The reverse field below the wreath bow has no visible O. That supports Philadelphia over 1839-O, which places the New Orleans mark beneath the wreath. The photograph establishes the 1839 date and early starred design. A specific die marriage has not been assigned. The weights and dimensions here describe the standard and the cataloged type.

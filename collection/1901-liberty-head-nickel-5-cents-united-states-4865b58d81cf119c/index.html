Historical background

01Why the V needed a word beneath it

Charles E. Barber’s Liberty Head nickel first appeared in 1883. The early reverse gave the value as V, with no word to explain what the five meant. Reports of people gilding nickels and trying to pass them as five-dollar gold pieces became part of the coin’s story. PCGS documents the reports and the revision to the reverse. The well-worn stories about individual con artists are harder to establish.

By 1901 the revised arrangement had been in use for years. The V still holds the center, but CENTS makes the denomination explicit. The old design problem is visible in the lettering even though the controversy was long past. Try reading the reverse from the center outward. First there is the V and its wreath, then the word that makes the numeral useful at a glance.

[1][2][3]

02From the shield to the portrait

The earlier Shield nickel in this collection presents its value as a large Arabic 5. Barber put a classical head of Liberty on the front and substituted the Roman V on the back. The design changed, but the 1901 piece still belongs to the copper-nickel five-cent denomination the United States introduced in 1866. Its nominal weight was five grams, like the Shield nickel. The different diameter and design are easier to see than the continuity in the metal standard.

PCGS records more than 26 million regular 1901 pieces struck at Philadelphia. That scale fits the coin’s purpose. A design that may look formal when enlarged was meant for the repeated, routine business of making change. The wear on this coin has flattened much of the portrait, but the date and the broad V still identify it.

[1][2][4]

03An ordinary issue in an eventful year

The date also places this coin in the year of the Pan-American Exposition in Buffalo. President William McKinley delivered a speech there on September 5, 1901, and was shot the next day. The nickel carries no design referring to the fair or to McKinley. Its date connects it to the period, while its imagery belongs to a series used across many years.

[1][5]

Identification notes

Date and mint

The date reads 1901, and the reverse has the wreath and V of the Liberty Head series. PCGS 3862 supplies the Philadelphia attribution for the regular issue. I have not assigned a proof attribution, die variety, or numerical grade from the photographs.

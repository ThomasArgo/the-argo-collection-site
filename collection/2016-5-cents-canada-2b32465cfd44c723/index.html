2016 · Canada · Elizabeth II · Royal Canadian Mint

The Royal Canadian Mint’s circular logo, with a stylized maple leaf rising from an M, appears beneath the portrait. The 2001 five-cent coin in this collection has a small P in that position. The beaver still occupies the same log, and the two pieces show how long a design can last through changes in alloy and portrait.

Historical background

01Why a nickel has so many materials

Canada’s early five-cent pieces were small silver coins. In 1922 a larger coin made mostly of nickel took their place. The Royal Canadian Mint’s specifications trace subsequent changes through wartime copper-zinc and plated steel issues, a return to nickel and a later copper-nickel standard. By 2016 the standard blank was predominantly steel. The name nickel had become a name for the amount, even when it no longer described most of the coin.

[1][2]

02One animal, many years

George Kruger Gray’s beaver first appeared on the denomination in 1937. It had already appeared on Canada’s first postage stamp, and the country made it an official national symbol in 1975. Its place in the fur trade helps explain that history. On the coin the animal is drawn close to the ground, with fine strokes for the fur and a broad tail breaking up the horizontal lines of the log.

The Mint used a different five-cent design for the 2017 Confederation anniversary. The ordinary 2016 reverse therefore makes a useful comparison with that special year. This piece is an example of the design people already knew before that change.

[2][5]

03The difference below the portrait

Compare the little marks on the two five-cent pieces in the collection. The earlier P identified plating. The later logo identifies the Royal Canadian Mint. The 2016 coin also has Susanna Blunt’s uncrowned portrait, first used in 2003, in place of Dora de Pédery-Hunt’s diademed bust. The beaver side did far more to preserve the resemblance between them.

[1][3][4]

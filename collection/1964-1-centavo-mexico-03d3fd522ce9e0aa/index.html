Historical background

01A hundredth of a peso in 1964

The Banco de México opened in 1925 after years of argument about who should issue the country’s money. Its own history traces the decision back through the Revolution, when competing note issues had made paper money hard to trust. A central bank and a national mint have different jobs. The mint made the coin and put its mark on it. The centavo took its value from the national peso.

By 1964 Mexico was in the period the central bank calls desarrollo estabilizador, or stabilizing development. The peso stood at 12.50 to the US dollar, a fixed rate that the central bank describes alongside growth and relatively stable prices. One centavo was the hundredth part of one of those pesos. The brass piece puts that national monetary history into the smallest denomination.

[1][2]

02The eagle and the place it landed

In the foundation story of Mexico-Tenochtitlan, the Mexica were looking for a sign that would tell them where to settle. The eagle on the cactus became the image through which that story was remembered. The snake is part of the familiar modern emblem, though accounts and earlier images vary. The basic grouping outlasted the city’s fall to the Spanish and became part of the imagery of the independent state.

The relief on this centavo is remarkably busy for a coin only about sixteen millimetres wide. The feathers and the coils of the snake are squeezed between the cactus below and the country’s full name above. The wreath closes the design at the bottom, leaving the entire other face free for the number and date.

[1][3]

03Reading the wheat side

Lorenzo Rafael’s design uses the wheat itself to divide the face. The number and its abbreviation sit high to either side of the stalk, while the date and mintmark sit low. Wheat is a recognizable crop, which makes an agricultural reading natural. I haven’t found a design brief linking this choice to a particular government program or harvest.

Identification notes

The earlier wheat centavo

The date, wheat layout and small mark at lower right agree with the 1964 Mᵒ listing. A later wheat centavo introduced in 1970 also carries a national eagle, wheat and a mintmark. Its official specifications belong to that later type. The 2.00 g and 16 mm figures on this page come from the catalog of the 1950–1969 series. I’ve kept the photo viewer’s original sequence and used the catalog’s face names in the descriptions. No individual measurements, die variety, or grade are assigned.

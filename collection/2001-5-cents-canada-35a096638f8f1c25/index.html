Historical background

01A trade animal on small change

The beaver had been a valuable part of the fur trade centuries before the five-cent coin appeared. Canadian Heritage traces the animal’s standing as a national symbol to that trade, in which beaver pelts were sought for the felt hat market. By the time Canada recognised the beaver as an official symbol in 1975, it had already appeared on a postage stamp, in city arms, and on this denomination.

George Kruger Gray’s coin design dates to 1937, the same redesign that put his maple twig on the cent. Here the animal is low to the ground, with its rounded back below the value and its tail stretching toward the rim. Two much smaller leaves flank the denomination. They leave most of the available space to the beaver.

[2][4]

02The metal under the beaver

Two ordinary beaver five-cent types were struck with the date 2001. One retained the 75 percent copper and 25 percent nickel alloy, with a standard weight of 4.60 g. The P-marked type used a plated steel blank and weighed 3.95 g by specification. The P records which construction was used. The catalog lists Winnipeg for circulation production, but the letter itself is a plating indicator rather than a city code.

The Mint’s process laid nickel, copper and nickel over the steel. The thickness of the layers could be adjusted to give a coin an electromagnetic signature that cash-handling equipment could recognise. The visible designs stayed familiar while the blank was redesigned for the machines that would take it.

Identification notes

The P and the beaver

The original shows a P below the portrait and the regular beaver reverse dated 2001. Together they support the plated KM 182b attribution. The Mint gives 21.2 mm for the plated type, while the date-specific Coins and Canada entry gives 21.1 mm. I’ve used the Mint’s specification and have left this coin’s measurements and strike finish open.

20 Euro Cents

2002 · Federal Republic of Germany · Federal Ministry of Finance · euro area

The Brandenburg Gate opens toward the viewer on Germany’s twenty-cent coin. In Berlin its reopening marked the end of a divided city. On this 2002 piece it shares space with the map used when twelve countries began paying with the same cash.

Historical background

01An open gate

Berlin’s Brandenburg Gate was once a city entrance. After the Berlin Wall went up, the gate stood in the border area and could no longer serve as a passage between the two sides. It reopened on 22 December 1989, weeks after the Wall opened. People could once again walk through an arch that had been a familiar sight even while it was out of reach.

Heinsdorff’s design draws the eye toward that passage. The pavement lines narrow toward the middle and the chariot and horses of the Quadriga sit above. The European Central Bank describes the open perspective as a way of linking German reunification to European unity. On the coin, the columns make a small but recognisable view of the monument.

[2][5]

02The day the euro reached the till

The euro had existed as an accounting and electronic currency since January 1999. People first received euro notes and coins in change on 1 January 2002, when twelve countries made the switch to euro cash. Banks, retailers and cash machines had to be stocked in advance. For someone used to paying in Deutsche Mark, the new coins carried a familiar German image but could be spent throughout the euro area.

The map on this example represents the European Union before its 2004 enlargement. That group had fifteen members, while twelve introduced euro cash in 2002. The common side puts the currency in a broader European setting than the list of countries that changed their cash on that first day. A later map used a wider geographic view of Europe.

[3][4][6]

03Seven indentations and one letter

The rim has seven shallow indentations in the outline the ECB calls the Spanish flower. That shape helps distinguish the twenty-cent coin from nearby denominations. Germany used five mint letters on its launch-year issues. On this piece the small F below 2002 places it in Stuttgart. A same-date example in the Berlin coin cabinet has the same design and outline with an A instead.

[1][3][4][7]

Identification notes

Stuttgart and the first map

The enlarged original shows an F below the date. The gate, twenty-cent denomination, shaped rim and early map match the 2002 Stuttgart listing. The photographs leave the die and strike finish open. The weight, diameter and thickness are ECB specifications for the denomination.

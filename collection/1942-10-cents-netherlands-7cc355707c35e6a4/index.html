Historical background

01A Dutch coin under occupation

German forces invaded on 10 May 1940. Wilhelmina and the government escaped to Britain, where she spoke to listeners back home on Radio Oranje. The mint in Utrecht was still operating, but the orders under which it worked now came from an administration subject to the occupiers.

The familiar denomination survived that upheaval. Ten cents was still one tenth of a gulden, commonly called a dubbeltje. As small change became scarce, the new zinc issues supplied coins people could use in ordinary transactions. The mint history describes their circulation alongside older money that had escaped hoarding. Someone receiving a new zinc piece in change could still have recognized its value immediately.

[3][4]

02What the flowers were meant to say

The three tulips seem an ordinary choice for a Dutch coin. In the design account quoted by mint historian L. M. J. Boegheim, however, Nico de Haas linked the three-part motif to an old rune and to ideas of strength and fertility for people and soil. That was the designer’s explanation. The coin gives no clue as to how the person spending it would have read those flowers.

Boegheim also describes an earlier ten-cent design that was rejected before release. That history matters when comparing photographs. The three tulip heads and the ribbon are specific to the adopted design, and the large 10 flanked by detached twigs is a second check. The photographed coin has both.

[1][4]

03Making change from zinc

The mint’s wartime files record shortages of metals important to German industry, including nickel and copper. The zinc program covered several denominations, so it took more than a single new design to keep cash transactions moving. The ten-cent piece remained a practical size. Its striped numeral and plain date still read clearly in the photo even where the field has darkened.

The date belongs to the die. It does not tell us when this particular coin reached a till or how long it was carried. What it does give us is a close link to the first period when the adopted zinc design was entering daily use.

[1][4]

Identification notes

The circulation design

The tulips, ribbon, detached twigs, and 1942 date agree with the cataloged zinc design. The Schulman photographs show a proof comparison, so they help with the design and date. I have left the photographed coin’s finish and die variety unassigned. Its surface marks are too indistinct to classify from these views. The listed measurements describe the type.

Historical background

01Two meanings of Regina

The name on the reverse is the name of a city. The same word on the obverse is a title. In the Latin legend beside Elizabeth II’s portrait, regina means queen. The city received its name in honor of Queen Victoria. On this dollar the same word thus looks in two directions, toward a particular place on the prairie and toward the monarchy under which it was named.

The dates 1882 and 1982 make the purpose of the issue clear. This is the centennial of the founding of Regina as a settler town. The site and the surrounding land had a history well before that date. The City of Regina describes the area as part of Indigenous homelands and records the name Pile of Bones. The skull on the reverse makes sense in light of that earlier name, while the large building behind it represents the city as a seat of government.

[1][3]

02How a railway stop became a capital

In 1882 Lieutenant-Governor Edgar Dewdney selected the site along the railway route. Regina’s own account notes that the choice was controversial because Dewdney owned nearby land. The decision was about far more than giving a town a name. The railway and territorial administration directed settlement and investment, while the reserve system Dewdney helped establish had lasting consequences for Indigenous communities.

The North-West Mounted Police moved their headquarters to Regina in 1882, and the city remained associated with police training. Saskatchewan became a province in 1905 and Regina its capital in 1906. The legislature on the coin makes that outcome visible. Huntley Brown places it above a much larger skull, so the symbols read at very different scales. The building is recognizable as a skyline, while the skull and horns take up most of the space the viewer notices first.

[3]

03A dollar made for the anniversary

The Royal Canadian Mint struck this silver-copper dollar for collectors. The same design was sold in proof and brilliant uncirculated finishes. On the photographed coin the open area around Elizabeth II looks reflective, and the portrait appears lighter. That contrast suggests proof, though reflections from the holder and the lighting make the two finishes hard to distinguish in these photographs.

Canada’s earlier silver dollar often carried Emanuel Hahn’s voyageur design. Here the reverse tells the story of a single city. Brown fits a building and a pair of sweeping horns into the same small space, and the two dates make it clear why those images belong together.

[1][2][4][5]

Identification notes

Finish and published specifications

The designs match the Bank of Canada Museum’s proof example. Numista assigns the issue to Ottawa and lists a standard weight of about 23.33 g and a diameter of 36 mm. GreatCollections gives 23.33 g and 36.07 mm for the type. The museum records 23.3 g and 35.9 mm for its own proof. I haven’t weighed or measured this coin, and its finish remains open.

Historical background

01How to read the date

The year is the first thing to check when identifying this coin, and the easiest thing to miss if you are looking for four Arabic numerals. The date is part of the Chinese inscription above the portrait. In the order it is meant to be read, 八十四 is eighty-four. The Republic of China calendar counts 1912 as year one, which puts year eighty-four in 1995. The number on the other side is a different piece of information. The large 1 tells you what the coin is worth, while the inscription around the portrait tells you when it was made.

The reverse writes the value twice, once as 壹圓 and again as 1. The form 壹 is the formal character for one. The characters read from right to left on this coin, just as the date does on the front. Setting a plain numeral below them makes the value immediately recognizable even to someone who cannot read the legend. The geometric border does the work that a picture might do on another coin by giving the denomination a distinctive frame.

[1][2]

02A dollar with a history

Taiwan’s Central Bank traces the New Taiwan dollar to a currency reform in June 1949. The Bank of Taiwan had issued the preceding Taiwan dollar beginning in 1946. After hyperinflation, the reform exchanged 40,000 old dollars for one new dollar. The change was a reset of the currency unit, which is why the name New Taiwan dollar matters even on a coin issued decades later.

By 1995 the one-dollar piece was ordinary small change. The date marks the year of this example rather than the beginning of the denomination. That distinction makes a long-lived circulation design useful to a collector. Coins that look nearly identical across a room can preserve a run of different issue years, and a small change to the characters around the portrait can distinguish them. The Central Bank’s official specification also explains the coin’s color. It is a copper-rich alloy with nickel and aluminium. The photographs show how this example looks today, while the official record tells us what the type was intended to contain.

[1][3]

03An older portrait at a turning point

Chiang Kai-shek’s portrait was already part of this denomination before the year on this coin. By 1995 Lee Teng-hui was president, and Taiwan was in the middle of constitutional changes. Martial law had ended in 1987. In March 1996 Lee won Taiwan’s first direct presidential election, a year after the date above Chiang’s portrait.

That overlap is what makes this small coin interesting. A circulating design can outlast the political era in which it was chosen. Its portrait tells you whom the design commemorates. To find out who actually held office when a particular coin was made, you still have to read the date. The two pieces of evidence fit together here without pointing to the same person.

[1][4][5]

Identification notes

Year and mint attribution

The final section of the portrait legend is 八十四年. The reverse confirms a dollar rather than five or ten dollars. The Central Mint attribution comes from the catalog entry for the issue. There is no mintmark identified in the two photographs. The finish and any finer production variety remain unassigned.

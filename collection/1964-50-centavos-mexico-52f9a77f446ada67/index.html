Historical background

01Cuauhtémoc and the siege

Cuauhtémoc succeeded Cuitláhuac after an outbreak of smallpox had killed his predecessor. He took charge of Mexico-Tenochtitlan in the final stage of the war. Hernán Cortés’s force depended on Indigenous allies, and the fighting cut off the island city from supplies. The defenders held out for nearly three months. On 13 August 1521 Cuauhtémoc was captured and the city fell.

His capture did not end his life immediately. Cortés later took him on an expedition toward Honduras and had him executed in 1525. The coin’s profile belongs to the much later public memory of the siege. By 1887, Mexico City had already erected a monument to Cuauhtémoc and the defenders of Tenochtitlan and Tlatelolco. The coin gives that remembered figure the intimacy of something handled every day.

[3][4][7]

02Two views of the same past

The headdress frames the face in repeated curved lines. Across the coin, the eagle and cactus recall the founding tradition of Tenochtitlan. The two sides can be read together. One evokes how the city began, and the other gives a face to its defense. The modern country’s name above the eagle makes clear who issued the coin.

[1][4][5]

03A smaller half peso

Earlier bronze 50-centavo pieces also carried Cuauhtémoc. That version was cataloged at 33.5 mm and 14 g. The end-of-1963 monetary reform specified a copper-nickel coin of 25 mm and 6.5 g. A familiar ruler and a familiar value carried over to a noticeably smaller piece, a change a person could feel even before reading the date.

[1][2][6]

Identification notes

Which Cuauhtémoc half peso?

The 1964 date, pale metal and the portrait and legend layout agree with KM 451. The mark beside the date supports the Mexico City attribution. The fine detail in the headdress leaves the smaller mark difficult to read independently. The photographs establish the type and date, and I’ve left the die variety, grade and specimen measurements open.

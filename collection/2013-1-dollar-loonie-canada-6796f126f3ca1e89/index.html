Historical background

01The bird that replaced a canoe

The Royal Canadian Mint had planned to use the familiar Voyageur design for the dollar coin that would replace the dollar note. In November 1986 its master dies went missing. The Mint turned to a design it already had, Robert-Ralph Carmichael’s common loon. The eleven-sided coin appeared in June 1987. The bird gave the dollar a name people could use as readily as the number on it.

Carmichael’s scene is quiet for a coin made to take the place of a banknote. The loon floats with only a few ripples around it, and the shoreline is low enough to leave open water and sky. The small initials near the right are easy to overlook.

[2][4]

02A change under the surface

In April 2012 Canada introduced a new generation of dollar and two-dollar circulation coins with extra security features. The loonie retained its shape and 26.5 mm diameter. The standard weight dropped from 7 g for the previous bronze-plated nickel type to 6.27 g for the brass-plated steel issue.

The circled maple leaf above the loon makes the change visible. It is a laser mark, separate from the engraving that shows the bird and shore. The photographed coin was dated the following year and shows the two elements together. The royal portrait remained Blunt’s, so the two sides preserve much of the familiar appearance even while the blank and its security changed.

Identification notes

The ordinary 2013 loon

The 2013 date, the swimming loon and the circle with a maple leaf match the ordinary security type. The mark identifies the generation, while the precise strike finish and mint facility of this specimen remain open. Weight and dimensions are Mint specifications, not measurements of the photographed coin.

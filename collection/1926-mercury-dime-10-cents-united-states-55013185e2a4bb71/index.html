Historical background

01The woman in the winged cap

The wings are the easiest part of the portrait to spot. They also explain the name Mercury dime. Viewers were reminded of the Roman god Mercury, although Weinman’s figure represents Liberty. The United States Mint describes the wings as a sign of freedom of thought. This coin was made ten years after the design first appeared in 1916, so the image was already a familiar part of everyday money by 1926.

The portrait sits high enough that the date, the motto, and the designer’s initials all fit around the neck. Notice the difference between those small marks and the large letters of LIBERTY. One gives the year, one identifies the designer, and one names the figure. The wing is what the eye catches first, but the lettering is what tells you how to read it.

[1][2]

02A dime for everyday change

A dime is one-tenth of a dollar, worth two five-cent pieces. In 1926 the dime was a small silver coin. The nickel in this collection from 1901 was larger and twice as heavy at the catalog standard, even though it was worth half as much. That comparison explains why size alone is an unreliable guide to value. The denominations belonged to different metal standards.

Turn the coin over and you see another contrast. A fasces, a bundle of rods around an axe, occupies the center. The olive branch bends around its straight lines. The two images invite a reading of strength alongside peace. On a coin this small, the branch and the bundle do most of the work before the motto and the denomination come into focus.

[1][2]

03What else was new in 1926

One event from the year on this coin survives in a very different sort of collection. The Library of Congress preserves the history files of the National Broadcasting Company, which began network broadcasting in November 1926. The dime was already an established design when radio networks were beginning to connect audiences across the country. The comparison is a reminder that the new things of 1926 and the familiar ones often sat side by side. The date on the coin helps put both in view.

[3]

Identification notes

Where to look for the mintmark

The date reads 1926. Look next to the lower end of the fasces, to the right of ONE. No distinct D or S is visible there. That supports the unmarked Philadelphia issue, PCGS 4954. A closer examination under angled light would make an absence easier to confirm. The worn bands have not been assigned a strike designation.

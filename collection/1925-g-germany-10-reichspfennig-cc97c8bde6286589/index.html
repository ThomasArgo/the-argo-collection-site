Historical background

01Small change after the paper mark

In the autumn of 1923 the prices people saw in German shops could outrun the pay they had just collected. The Bundesbank traces the crisis back through wartime borrowing, the end of gold redemption and postwar deficits. In November 1923 the Rentenmark offered a stable unit again. The Reichsmark followed in 1924, after measures that included a credit squeeze and an effort to rebuild gold reserves.

One hundred Reichspfennig made a Reichsmark. By 1925 it made sense to put a two-digit value on a coin that might stay in a pocket for days. The number on this piece is useful in a way that the huge figures on the last inflation notes could no longer be.

[2][3]

02Oak on one side, grain on the other

The inscription still calls the state Deutsches Reich, the official name used under the republic. The layout keeps the denomination in the center rather than setting it beneath a ruler’s portrait. On the other face the stalks cross into a compact pattern that leads the eye toward the date. Those are observable design choices. The grain is an apt image for money meant to buy everyday goods, although that connection is a reading of the design.

Identification notes

The Karlsruhe mark

The G is readable above the grain, and the date reads 1925. The catalog lists the issue for several mints in the same year. The G is what narrows this example to Karlsruhe. The listed alloy, diameter, weight and edge are type data. This specimen was not tested or measured.

Historical background

01Ten cents in a year remembered for stocks

The Federal Reserve’s history dates the start of the downturn to August 1929. The dramatic stock market losses came in October, and banking crises followed in later years. Those events often get compressed into a single story about the crash. This dime gives us a year but no month, so there is no basis for placing its manufacture or first use before or after the October sell-off.

The dime belonged to the everyday side of that economy. Stocks were bought and sold at prices that could move sharply in a single day. This coin had a stated value of ten cents throughout. What that amount meant to the person spending it depended on prices, wages, and whether they had work. The Federal Reserve’s account describes falling demand and rising unemployment as the contraction spread. The coin is a concrete reminder of a distinction that is easy to lose in charts. The number on a piece of money can stay fixed while the circumstances of the people using it change.

[3][4]

02What stayed on the dime

The Mint kept Weinman’s Winged Liberty design. The cap’s wings, interpreted by the Mint as freedom of thought, are still distinct in the photograph. The reverse leaves no ambiguity about its purpose. ONE DIME frames the lower edge, while an olive branch curls up against the vertical bundle of rods. The denomination is as much a part of the composition as the symbols.

The 1926 and 1929 dimes are useful to compare because the design and type specifications agree. The dates and the surfaces are particular to the individual coins. The scratches in the reverse field on this piece, for example, help tell the two photographs apart. They do not identify the place it was made or the circumstances in which it was spent.

[1][2]

03Liberty’s last years on the dime

Weinman’s dime continued through the Depression and the Second World War. The Mint retired the design after 1945 and issued the Roosevelt dime in January 1946. Its account connects the choice of denomination to Franklin Roosevelt’s support for the March of Dimes, the effort to raise funds for polio research. That later association gave the denomination a new public meaning. Nothing about the winged portrait on the 1929 issue tells you that change is coming. The design belongs to a longer stretch of time than the date at the bottom, which is one reason I like looking at a pair of these dimes together.

[1]

Identification notes

Philadelphia comparison

The date is clear. I cannot see a distinct mint letter in the usual spot by the base of the fasces near ONE. The comparison is therefore Philadelphia, PCGS 4972, with that attribution based on the photograph. PCGS lists Full Bands separately under 4973. I haven’t applied that designation or a grade to this piece.

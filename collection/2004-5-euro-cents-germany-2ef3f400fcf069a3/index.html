Historical background

01An oak branch after the mark

When the Deutsche Mark gave way to euro cash in January 2002, Germany chose images for its own side of the coins. Rolf Lederbogen’s oak twig recalls the branch on German pfennigs. The lowest three euro denominations kept that visual connection, while the ten-, twenty- and fifty-cent pieces used the Brandenburg Gate.

The branch on this coin spreads almost to the ring of stars. Its lobed leaves and acorns are distinct at this scale, with room near the bottom for the date and mint letter. The old pfennig and the new cent belong to different currencies, but a person familiar with the earlier change could recognise the motif.

[2][5]

02A globe in a copper-coloured field

On the common side, Luc Luycx put the number on the left and a globe on the right. The globe puts Europe in relation to two neighbouring continents. The larger denominations used a different treatment of the map. These two faces let the coin be recognisably German and recognisably part of the shared currency at the same time.

[1][4][5]

03Five mints, one design

Germany distributed production among Berlin, Munich, Stuttgart, Karlsruhe and Hamburg. The Bundesbank records their letters as A, D, F, G and J. The mark takes up very little space, but it gives otherwise similar coins a traceable place of manufacture. The copper-coloured surface is a coating over steel, and the smooth edge is part of the denomination’s standard specification.

[1][3][4]

Identification notes

Reading the mark

The original photographs show G, the 2004 date and the five-cent globe. That combination matches Karlsruhe. The photographs do not establish a die or strike finish. The dimensions are for the standard issue.

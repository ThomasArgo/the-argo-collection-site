Historical background

01A different kind of likeness

Ian Rank-Broadley’s portrait was introduced in 1998. The Royal Mint Museum contrasts it with the more idealised likeness by Raphael Maklouf. Rank-Broadley wanted the head to fill more of the surface, a practical choice on a coin only about twenty millimetres wide. In this photograph the cheek, hair and neckline sit close to the inscription. The sculptor’s initials occupy the small space below.

[1][3]

02A gate from Westminster

A portcullis is a barred gate. The crowned version became the emblem used by Parliament, and Christopher Ironside adapted it for the decimal penny. At first the coin said NEW PENNY, a useful distinction when people were learning the new values. By 1982 the word NEW had been dropped. The gate and numeral were still in place in 2006, shortly before the Royal Shield redesign arrived in 2008.

[2][4][5]

03The copper colour stayed

The earlier bronze penny and the standard 2006 steel penny have the same official diameter and weight. The Mint changed to copper-plated steel in 1992 in response to metal prices. The coating kept the familiar colour and the core made the coin magnetic. The two pennies in the collection illustrate how much can change without changing the amount a coin pays. Both came from the mint’s decimal era in South Wales.

[1][2][6]

Identification notes

The circulation pattern

The date, IRB and portcullis match the standard 2006 type. A silver proof with the same design also exists. This coin’s copper-coloured surface supports the steel circulation-type attribution. The core and finish have not been tested.

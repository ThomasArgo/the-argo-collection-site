c. AD 330–335 · Roman Empire · Constantine I’s reign · Constantine II as Caesar

Historical background

01The young Caesar

Constantine II was made Caesar in 317 while still a child. The appointment placed him in his father’s plans for succession long before he could govern alone. By the middle of the 330s he had responsibilities in the West. His coins gave him a public identity alongside the senior ruler.

The IVN in the legend marks him as the younger Constantine. The last letters give him the title Caesar. Those details matter for dating the coin. He took the title Augustus after Constantine I died in 337. The legend and two standards together point to the earlier part of his career.

[1][3][5]

02Glory of the army

The soldiers frame two tall standards. Even with the worn surface, the decorated tops and central poles are distinct from the spears on the outside. The legend gives the army the glory, while the portrait connects it to the young Caesar. Similar designs were struck for several members of the imperial family. The wording on the portrait side does much of the work of identifying him.

Excavated coins at Corinth include versions with two standards and versions with one. The two standards here are clearly visible. The little mint letters below are harder to separate from the edge, which limits how closely the piece can be assigned.

[1][2][4]

03The succession broke the partnership

After Constantine I died, his three sons took the title Augustus. Constantine II received western territories including Britain, Gaul and Spain. His relationship with Constans broke down and he invaded Italy in 340. He was killed near Aquileia. The coin’s matching soldiers belong to the earlier public image of a dynasty that still had a senior ruler at its head.

[3][5]

Identification notes

A good type match and an uncertain mint

The bust, apparent junior Caesar legend and two standards match the type represented by London Museum N906. Below the soldiers I can make out a few raised strokes, but I cannot separate them into a reliable sequence of letters. Published two-standard examples carry marks as different as SMANS at Antioch and TRP* or TRS* at Trier. Neither reading can be established here, and the comparison leaves the mint, workshop and exact RIC entry open. The displayed measurements belong to N906.

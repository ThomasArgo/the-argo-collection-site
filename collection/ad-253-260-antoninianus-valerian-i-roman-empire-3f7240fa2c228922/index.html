Antoninianus (Valerian I)

AD 253–260 · Valerian I

Turn this coin over and you’ll see Valerian holding a globe, hailed as the restorer of humankind. He and his son Gallienus ruled together during one of Rome’s most turbulent decades. In AD 260, the Sasanian king Shapur I captured Valerian near Edessa.

The mint and exact variant are provisional.

Obverse and reverse

Obverse

IMP C P LIC VALERIANVS AVG

Imperator Caesar Publius Licinius Valerianus Augustus

Valerian faces right. Look for the points of his crown above his brow, its ties behind his neck, and the folds of his cloak across his shoulder. The full inscription is reconstructed where the rim is worn.

[1]

Reverse

RESTITVT GENER HVMANI

To the restorer of humankind

Valerian strides to the right with one hand raised and a small globe in his left. Much of the lettering survives along the upper edge. The full reading draws on a closely matching coin.

01The emperor behind the coin

Valerian came from a Roman senatorial family and was already an experienced commander when he became emperor in AD 253. The emperor Trebonianus Gallus had sent him to raise troops against Aemilianus, a rival backed by his army. Gallus was killed before Valerian could reach him. As Valerian approached, Aemilianus’s own soldiers killed him too. In a single year, armies had twice decided who would rule Rome.

Valerian made his adult son Gallienus co-emperor. With two rulers in the field, Gallienus could deal with threats on the Rhine and in the western provinces while Valerian went east. Their coins made the partnership visible. Each had his own portrait and titles, but both could appear as the ruler who would put the Roman world back in order.

[3][4]

02The war with Shapur

By the time Valerian took power, Shapur I of the Sasanian Empire had already fought Rome under Gordian III and Philip the Arab. His forces pushed into Syria. Control of cities, garrisons, and routes through northern Mesopotamia mattered to both sides. In AD 260 the two rulers came up against each other near Edessa and Carrhae.

Shapur recorded Valerian’s defeat and capture in a three-language inscription at the Kaʿba-ye Zardosht. It was a king’s account of his own victory. Other accounts describe an attempted negotiation, so exactly how Valerian fell into Shapur’s hands is uncertain. He died in captivity. Later stories about how he was treated are harder to substantiate.

[3][4][5]

03Rome after Edessa

Gallienus stayed in power after his father’s capture, but he faced rivals on several fronts. Postumus built a rival empire around the Rhine and Gaul. In the east, commanders tied to Valerian’s army backed rival emperors, while Odaenathus of Palmyra became a military power in his own right. Each region had its own troubles. The fact that they came to a head around the same time shows how much Rome relied on local commanders and the loyalty of their troops.

Shapur made the capture part of how he presented his reign. A cameo discussed by the Metropolitan Museum of Art has been interpreted as showing the encounter. It makes an interesting companion to the coin. Here, Valerian holds the globe. In the Sasanian images of his capture, he is the figure through whom Shapur shows his own power.

[3][4][6]

04The coin and its silver

Caracalla introduced the antoninianus early in the third century. On coins of this denomination, the emperor wears a crown with rays. It is often understood to have been worth two denarii, although its exact value is debated. By Valerian’s time it had become a mainstay of Roman coinage, even as the amount of silver used in it declined at different rates from issue to issue.

A coin can look silvery without containing much silver throughout. Studies of third-century coins show that silver could be concentrated at the surface. Burial and corrosion can change what that surface looks like today. That is one reason photographs alone cannot tell us how much silver is in this coin.

[1][2][7][8]

05Inscriptions and symbolism

Look at Valerian’s raised hand on the reverse. The gesture recalls an emperor addressing his troops. The globe and the words around it give the image a much wider reach. The inscription calls him the restorer of humankind, without tying the claim to one place or one victory. Similar language appeared on coins of both Valerian and Gallienus.

The closest dated example was struck before the capture. That makes this design a window into what Valerian could still promise at the time. A coin was a small thing to carry such a large claim, but it could put that claim in the hands of people who would never see the emperor.

[1][2]

Identification notes

Mint and dating

The radiate bust facing right, the advancing figure, and the letters around GENER closely match Ashmolean HCR8487. The Ashmolean assigns its coin to Antioch and dates it to AD 254–256 following Göbl. The related RIC 220 example in Judaism and Rome is assigned to Viminacium and reads RESTITVTI with a final I. Antioch and a date in the mid-250s are the best working attribution for this coin.

[1][2]

Bust and legend

The Ashmolean lists its example as Göbl MIR 36, 1564a and Cunetio 820. It treats it as a variant of RIC V.1 220 because the portrait is both draped and cuirassed. On this coin the shoulder and rim are worn, so the exact bust subtype and full lettering remain uncertain. The photographs support the Valerian antoninianus identification. The proposed RIC V.1 220A is still unconfirmed.

The weight, diameter, and metal listed below describe the Ashmolean example. This coin’s weight, diameter, and composition remain unmeasured.

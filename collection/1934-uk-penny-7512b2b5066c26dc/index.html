Historical background

01A large coin for one penny

Twelve pennies made a shilling and twenty shillings made a pound. That arithmetic helps explain why the large ONE PENNY is worth reading before guessing from the size. This piece is physically imposing for one of the smallest units people used every day.

The Royal Mint Museum traces the penny through earlier silver issues to the large base-metal coins. Britannia gave the bronze penny a familiar face through successive reigns. Compare her outline with the Britannia on the George II halfpenny in the collection. Both carry a shield, but the pose and engraving belong to very different generations.

[1][2]

02The king’s titles outlasted the gold standard

George V came to the throne in 1910. The words IND:IMP: make the British Empire part of an object as ordinary as a penny. The smaller portrait leaves a broad field between the king and the rim, while those titles continue right around it.

Britain left the gold standard in 1931. In 1934 the penny still had its familiar value and appearance. The Bank of England’s chronology puts the change in the wider setting of the Depression. These two timelines are visible together here, a familiar coin design carrying a date from a changed monetary system.

[1][3][4]

03Why the head became smaller

The Mint reduced the portrait in 1928 to address a practical problem. The earlier head displaced enough metal during striking to leave a faint echo on the Britannia side, an effect known as ghosting. The smaller portrait is therefore a clue to how the penny was made as well as to its date.

The Mint Museum also explains the striking contrast with 1933. Banks had enough pennies in stock that year and no general-circulation issue was needed. The catalog records nearly fourteen million for 1934. A change of one digit can mean a very different production story even when the design stays familiar.

[1][3]

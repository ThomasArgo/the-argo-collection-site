Historical background

01Liberty in motion

Adolph A. Weinman’s design had already been in use for nearly a quarter century by the time this coin was struck. Liberty walks toward a sun just breaking over the horizon. Her hand reaches out ahead of her while the flag and her drapery stream behind. Look for the rays coming up from the lower left. They fan out at an angle different from the folds of the fabric, so the whole composition has a sense of direction.

The reverse answers that movement with a bird planted firmly on a ledge. The eagle’s feathers fill almost the entire field, and the lettering has to fit around the wings. Even the small spaces around the rock are used. There is room for the mintmark on one side and the designer’s monogram on the other. These are easy to overlook until the coin is enlarged, but they give two different pieces of information about the object: who designed it and where it was made.

[1][2]

02An American coin dated 1940

The design dates to 1916. By the time this half dollar was made, images of the flag and the eagle had already passed through the First World War, the 1920s and the Depression. They acquired a new setting in 1940. The United States had not entered the Second World War, yet the Roosevelt administration was already finding ways to aid Britain and other countries fighting Germany.

The 1939 Neutrality Act allowed belligerents to buy American war material on cash-and-carry terms. Britain had to pay and arrange transport. In September 1940 the United States agreed to supply destroyers in exchange for access to bases. Lend-Lease followed in 1941, before American entry into the war that December. The half dollar was a regular issue, so the date records the year of manufacture rather than a special war commemoration. That difference is part of its interest. Ordinary money can carry the date of a turning point without carrying an image of the event.

[1][4]

03Fifty cents and a second life for the design

Two of these coins made a dollar. A half dollar had ten times the face value of a nickel and was still a substantial silver coin, weighing 12.5 grams at the type standard. Its value in a transaction came from its denomination. A collector examining one now can also consider its silver content and its condition, two things the person spending it did not have to work out at the counter.

The Walking Liberty half dollar ended in 1947. The design later returned on the American Silver Eagle, introduced in 1986. The two pieces are easy to separate when you turn them over. This coin still has Weinman’s eagle and the words HALF DOLLAR, and it belongs to the original circulating series. Its date and mintmark are a better starting point for identification than the familiar figure on the front alone.

[1][2][5]

Identification notes

The S below the branch

The obverse reads 1940. The small raised S just left of the ledge identifies San Francisco. PCGS catalogs the 1940-S regular strike as 6610. Its series guide gives a diameter of 30.6 mm, while its date entry gives 30.00 mm. Neither figure is a measurement of this coin. The photographs show wear, but I haven’t assigned a grade.

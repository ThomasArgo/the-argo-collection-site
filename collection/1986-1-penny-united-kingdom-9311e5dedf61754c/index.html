A crowned gate and a single numeral tell you almost everything you need to spend this penny. Turn it over and the portrait gives it a more precise place in time. It was struck in Wales in the second year of Raphael Maklouf’s portrait of Elizabeth II.

Historical background

01When a penny became a hundredth

Before Decimal Day, 240 old pennies made a pound. From 15 February 1971, a pound was divided into 100 new pence. The name penny survived, but it belonged to a different unit. The first decimal pennies said NEW PENNY so that anyone paying or making change could tell which system the coin belonged to. In 1982 the Mint dropped NEW. By 1986 the short inscription in the photograph was enough.

Preparing that change took a new mint. The Royal Mint began striking decimal bronze at Llantrisant in 1968, building up supplies while Tower Hill was still working. London struck its final coin in 1975. The 1986 penny belongs to the Welsh chapter of the Mint’s history, even though its reverse still recalls Westminster.

[2][5]

02The queen and the gate

Maklouf had to make a recognisable face in relief shallow enough to strike on a coin. The diadem and pearls pick out the profile without taking up much space. Compare the bare outline of Mary Gillick’s laureate portrait on the 1956 sixpence in this collection. The later portrait relies on quite different shapes to identify the same person.

A portcullis is a barred gate of the kind found at a castle entrance. The crowned version is a royal badge and the principal emblem used by Parliament. On the penny its chains hang in long curves around the square grille. Ironside had room for both the emblem and a prominent numeral that could be read at a glance.

[1][2][3][4][6][7]

03Before the steel core

The brown surface belongs to the bronze period of decimal pennies. In 1992 rising metal prices led the Royal Mint to introduce a copper-plated steel penny. The color could stay familiar while the material underneath changed. The 1986 date fixes this example on the bronze side of that change, even without a magnet test.

[2]

Identification notes

Matching an exact-date working strike

The date, Maklouf portrait and ONE PENNY portcullis agree with Museums Victoria NU 29779, an exact-date working strike. The visible features support the type. They leave the specimen’s die and finish open. The dimensions are the Royal Mint’s figures for the bronze issue.
